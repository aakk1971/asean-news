English presenter script

Good evening. We open tonight's bulletin with a look ahead to what promises to be the most consequential ASEAN summit in years. The Philippines will host the 49th ASEAN Summit and Related Summits from 15 to 18 November at the Philippine International Convention Center in Pasay City, Manila. Under the theme "Navigating Our Future, Together," the summit will focus on peace and security, economic prosperity and people empowerment. More than 17 world leaders have confirmed attendance, including US President Donald Trump and Brazilian President Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva.

The landmark Digital Economy Framework Agreement — DEFA — described as the world's first binding regional digital economy pact, is targeted for signing at the summit. ASEAN's Secretary-General has projected that the bloc's digital economy could reach 2 trillion US dollars by 2030, and DEFA is the regulatory architecture intended to harmonise rules on cross-border data flows, digital payments, e-commerce, cybersecurity and AI governance across the now 11 member states, following Timor-Leste's formal admission in October 2025.

The Department of Foreign Affairs confirmed today that Trump's late arrival — expected half an hour before the closing ceremonies — will not affect the summit programme. ASEAN leaders have agreed to extend the schedule to accommodate bilateral meetings with the US President. The Philippines will formally hand over the ASEAN chairship to Singapore on 17 November, and President Marcos is scheduled to hold bilateral meetings with the leaders of Australia, Brazil, Japan and South Korea on the summit sidelines.

Our analysis: The DEFA signing would represent the summit's most consequential economic deliverable, but the gap between aspiration and implementation is vast. ASEAN's current digital economy is estimated at roughly 300 billion US dollars — reaching the 2 trillion dollar target by 2030 requires not just a signed agreement but harmonised regulations, cross-border payment infrastructure and digital identity systems that do not yet exist at scale. Trump's presence is particularly significant: his tariff policies have reshaped trade flows across Southeast Asia, and the summit provides a meeting opportunity with ASEAN leaders navigating the US-China trade realignment in real time. The handover to Singapore — ASEAN's most economically advanced member and a hub for the AI hardware cycle — positions the bloc's 2027 agenda around digital and technology governance. Whether Manila delivers substance alongside spectacle will define the Philippines' legacy as ASEAN chair.

Good evening. We continue with market news from Indonesia, where the Jakarta Composite Index extended its rally for a second consecutive session. The IHSG closed 1.69 per cent higher at 5,339.4 on 13 November, building on the previous session's 2.66 per cent surge — a two-day cumulative gain of approximately 4.4 per cent from the 5,115 level. The LQ45 blue-chip index advanced 1.3 per cent. All sectors finished in positive territory on 12 November, led by the industrial sector at 4.53 per cent, and the momentum carried into Friday's session. Trading value on 12 November reached 11.98 trillion rupiah.

In a separate but closely related development, the Indonesia Stock Exchange expanded its High Shareholder Concentration reform — known as HSC — increasing the number of classified stocks from 83 to 383, representing approximately 6.16 per cent of IHSG's market capitalisation. The expanded list took effect today. The HSC Committee revised its methodology by dropping the 5 trillion rupiah market capitalisation screening threshold, meaning all listed stocks are now assessed. A stock is classified as HSC when 10 or fewer shareholders control 92.5 per cent or more of its shares. IDX President Director Jeffrey Hendrik stated that sanctions will apply if inaccurate shareholding reports are found.

Meanwhile, the Indonesian rupiah weakened 0.26 per cent to 18,216 per US dollar, near its record low of 18,279. Market analysts cited the positive MSCI rebalancing outcome, resolution of political uncertainty surrounding Vice President Gibran's resignation, and expectations of guaranteed rice supply as supporting factors for equities. However, the rupiah's continued weakness near record lows — despite the equity rally — indicates currency markets are pricing in broader macroeconomic risks that the stock market's technical bounce has not yet addressed.

Our analysis: The two-day rally is the IHSG's strongest consecutive-day performance in months, but context is critical. The index remains down approximately 37 per cent year-to-date from its all-time high above 8,500, and the rally has not been accompanied by a decisive shift in foreign fund flows. The HSC expansion to 383 stocks is the IDX's most aggressive governance intervention in its history, explicitly designed to address MSCI's transparency concerns ahead of the broader reclassification consultation that continues into 2027. By removing the market-cap threshold, the exchange is signalling that no stock is too large to be scrutinised for ownership concentration — a direct response to criticism that Indonesia's blue-chip index was inflated by thinly traded, concentrated-ownership stocks. The risk is that the aggressive reform pace creates short-term liquidity disruption, though the 6.16 per cent market-cap impact suggests the most systemically important stocks are not on the HSC list.

English presenter script

Good evening. We turn now to a landmark trade milestone from Vietnam. Vietnam's total merchandise trade reached approximately 1.01 trillion US dollars in the first 10 months of 2026, surpassing the trillion-dollar mark for the first time in the country's history — a tenfold increase from just over 100 billion US dollars when Vietnam joined the WTO in 2007. Total trade grew 32.3 per cent year-on-year. Exports totalled 499.24 billion dollars, up 27.5 per cent, while imports reached 511.3 billion, up 37.4 per cent. The trade balance shifted to a deficit of 12.07 billion, compared with a surplus of approximately 19.5 billion in the same period of 2025.

In October alone, total trade was 122 billion dollars, up 47.4 per cent year-on-year, with exports of 64.67 billion and imports of 57.36 billion, producing a monthly surplus of 7.3 billion — the second consecutive monthly surplus. The foreign-invested sector dominated exports at 405.3 billion, or 81.2 per cent of the total, up 33 per cent, while the domestic sector contributed 93.9 billion, or 18.8 per cent, up 8.2 per cent. Thirty-seven export items exceeded 1 billion US dollars, and eight exceeded 10 billion.

The United States remained Vietnam's largest export market at 159.4 billion dollars, while China was the largest import source at 212.46 billion. Registered foreign direct investment surged 76.4 per cent to 50.36 billion, with disbursed FDI rising 12.1 per cent to 21.07 billion. Deputy Prime Minister Nguyen Van Thang stated that achieving the full-year GDP growth target of 10 per cent would require approximately 12.5 per cent growth in the fourth quarter.

Our analysis: Vietnam's trillion-dollar trade milestone cements its position as Southeast Asia's most trade-intensive economy relative to GDP, but the composition reveals structural vulnerabilities that the headline obscures. The foreign-invested sector accounts for 81 per cent of exports, meaning Vietnam's trade success is overwhelmingly a story of multinational factory output rather than domestic industrial capacity. The shift from a 19.5 billion dollar surplus to a 12 billion dollar deficit reflects the rising cost of manufacturing inputs — particularly from China, where Vietnam's import bill surged to 212 billion, creating a deficit of nearly 135 billion with its northern neighbour alone. This dependency means Vietnam's trade performance is essentially a function of the global electronics supply chain that runs through Chinese components into Vietnamese assembly plants and onward to US consumers. The 10 per cent GDP target requiring 12.5 per cent Q4 growth appears extremely ambitious, suggesting the government may be signalling aspirational rather than achievable goals.

English presenter script

Good evening. We turn now to Malaysia, where third-quarter growth has beaten expectations. Malaysia's economy expanded 5.3 per cent year-on-year in Q3 2026, according to advance estimates from the Department of Statistics Malaysia, moderating from 6.0 per cent in Q2 but coming in at the upper end of Bloomberg's 4.5 to 5.4 per cent forecast range and above UOB's 4.5 per cent projection. On a quarter-on-quarter basis, growth actually accelerated to 4.6 per cent from 1.9 per cent in Q2. Year-to-date growth for the first three quarters stood at 5.5 per cent, compared with 4.8 per cent in the same period of 2025.

The sectoral breakdown shows broad-based strength. Manufacturing grew 7.5 per cent, driven by electrical, electronic and optical products, and petroleum, chemical, rubber and plastic products. Services expanded 5.4 per cent, led by wholesale and retail trade, information and communication, and finance and insurance. Construction grew 6.0 per cent, driven by non-residential buildings and specialised construction. Agriculture expanded 1.3 per cent on the back of oil palm and livestock. The sole weak spot was mining and quarrying, which contracted 4.9 per cent due to lower crude oil and condensate output.

Looking at the trade picture, Malaysia's trade was projected to surge 27.2 per cent to 3.9 trillion ringgit in 2026, after recording a historic 3 trillion ringgit in 2025 — the 28th consecutive year of trade surplus. The IMF forecast Malaysia's GDP growth at 5.2 per cent for 2026, moderating to 4.7 per cent in 2027.

Our analysis: Malaysia's 5.3 per cent growth confirms it as one of ASEAN's strongest performers this year, trailing only Vietnam's near-double-digit pace and comfortably ahead of Thailand's 2.4 to 2.7 per cent range. The manufacturing sector's 7.5 per cent expansion, driven by electronics and semiconductor-related products, underscores Malaysia's position as a key node in the global AI hardware supply chain — the same cycle lifting Singapore. The quarter-on-quarter acceleration to 4.6 per cent from 1.9 per cent suggests the year-on-year moderation reflects base effects rather than genuine deceleration. The mining contraction of 4.9 per cent is a structural headwind that Malaysia's diversification into electronics is progressively offsetting. The critical vulnerability remains Malaysia's exposure to potential US tariff escalation targeting ASEAN's role in China-plus-one supply chain rerouting.

English presenter script

Good evening. We continue in Thailand, where a significant change to the country's manufacturing data has altered the economic picture. Thailand's Office of Industrial Economics updated the Manufacturing Production Index to incorporate high-technology categories including laptops, optical transceivers and server UPS units, and adopted chain-linking methodology with annual weight updates. The revision resolved a longstanding discrepancy between strong export values and contracting manufacturing indicators, raising the 2026 baseline GDP growth projection from 2.4 per cent to 2.7 per cent. Growth in 2027 is forecast at 3.0 per cent, and ttb analytics projects that GDP could reach 3.7 to 4.0 per cent over the next five years under favourable policy execution.

Global demand for AI-related electronics has grown nearly fourfold over five to six years, and Thailand has positioned itself as a production hub for printed circuit boards, power supply units, hard disk drives and semiconductor parts — supported primarily by Taiwanese foreign direct investment. The Joint Standing Committee on Commerce, Industry and Banking lifted its 2026 growth outlook to 2.4 to 2.6 per cent.

However, the Bank of Thailand governor warned that the economy exhibits a pronounced K-shaped recovery. While AI-linked electronics export at record volumes, textiles, traditional manufacturing, tourism businesses dependent on Chinese visitors, and regional SMEs continue to struggle. Approximately 76,000 registered firms — 16 per cent of active companies — are classified as zombie SMEs with stagnant revenues unable to cover debt servicing costs. SMEs collectively employ roughly 12 million workers. The Thai-US Treasury yield spread is at its widest in two decades, creating capital outflow pressure and baht volatility.

Our analysis: The GDP revision is essentially a statistical correction that makes official data match a reality the market had already priced in: the AI electronics cycle is generating genuine economic value that the old MPI was not capturing. The upgrade from 2.4 to 2.7 per cent is meaningful for credibility, but Thailand's growth — even at the revised level — trails every major ASEAN peer except Myanmar and Laos. The K-shaped divergence is the most concerning feature: a thin slice of high-tech manufacturing is performing exceptionally well, while the broad domestic economy employing most workers is stagnating. The 76,000 zombie SMEs representing 16 per cent of active firms is a structural crisis that monetary policy cannot solve — the Bank of Thailand has already cut rates to 1 per cent with limited effect. The AI bubble risk flagged by analysts is real: a sharp correction in global AI investment would hit Thailand's best-performing sector disproportionately hard.

Good evening. We close tonight's bulletin with a significant monetary policy decision from Singapore. The Monetary Authority of Singapore kept the rate of appreciation, width and centre of the Singapore dollar nominal effective exchange rate policy band unchanged at its October 2026 review, surprising all 10 analysts polled by Reuters who had expected tightening. The policy band retains its appreciating slope, which MAS characterised as a pause in tightening rather than an easing.

MAS narrowed its 2026 core inflation forecast to 1.5 to 2.0 per cent, with core and headline inflation expected to remain slightly elevated in the coming months before easing more noticeably from mid-2027 as import costs ebb. Risks to inflation remain tilted to the upside, mainly from a possible sharp, sustained rise in energy or food prices. Singapore's advance Q3 2026 GDP came in at 5.4 per cent year-on-year — beating the 5.0 per cent consensus — and 1.1 per cent quarter-on-quarter seasonally adjusted.

MAS expects the economy to expand at a firm pace in the near term, then moderate as the AI cycle slows and global financial conditions tighten. It projects AI-related activity to slow in the second half of 2027, with the positive output gap narrowing through that year. Downside risks include a larger-than-expected rise in interest rates or a sharper pullback in AI investment. The government upgraded its full-year 2026 GDP growth forecast to 4.5 to 5.5 per cent, up from the previous range of 2.0 to 4.0 per cent.

Our analysis: The MAS decision to hold — against unanimous expectations of tightening — is the most significant dovish surprise in Singapore monetary policy in recent years. It signals that the central bank is choosing patience over pre-emption, betting that AI-driven inflation pressures will prove transitory as the cycle matures. This is a calculated gamble: Singapore's economy is growing at 5.4 per cent with a positive output gap, conditions that would normally warrant tighter policy. MAS is essentially making a forward-looking call that the AI boom will cool naturally by the second half of 2027. The upgraded full-year forecast of 4.5 to 5.5 per cent represents growth roughly double the rate initially anticipated — a remarkable outcome driven by the global AI and electronics hardware cycle. The weakness in accommodation and food services at just 2.1 per cent reminds us that the domestic consumption economy has not kept pace with export-oriented sectors. Energy remains the key swing factor: a renewed jump in crude linked to Gulf escalation could bring tightening back at the April 2027 review.
