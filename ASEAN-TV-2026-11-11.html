English presenter script

Good evening. We turn now to the Philippines with this report. The 2026 ASEAN Inclusive Growth Summit opened in Manila on 11 November, convening business leaders, government officials and civil society representatives under the theme "Resilient and Rising" ahead of the 49th ASEAN Summit scheduled for 15 to 17 November. The summit was organised by the Mastercard Center for Inclusive Growth in partnership with the ASEAN Business Advisory Council Philippines. Featured speakers included Teresita Sy-Coson, Vice Chairperson of SM Investments Corporation; Jaime Augusto Zobel de Ayala, Chairman of Ayala Corporation; Nitin Gajria, Regional Managing Director of Google; Sandhya Devanathan, Regional Managing Director of OpenAI; Jane Sun, CEO of Trip.com Group; Kuok Hui Kwong, Chairman and CEO of Shangri-La Group; Jon Huntsman, Vice Chairman of Mastercard; and Joey Concepcion, Chairman of ASEAN-BAC Philippines. Actress and UNHCR Special Envoy Angelina Jolie and Filipino Broadway star Lea Salonga also addressed the gathering. Agenda topics included digital transformation and AI in Southeast Asia, cybersecurity, financial health for micro, small and medium enterprises, sustainable tourism, and the creator economy. Officials highlighted that ASEAN's 700 million people and 70 million MSMEs represent one of the world's largest untapped digital markets. The summit serves as a prelude to the main ASEAN Summit where the Digital Economy Framework Agreement is expected to be signed.

Our analysis: The corporate lineup — spanning Mastercard, Google, OpenAI, Shangri-La, Trip.com and ASEAN's two largest conglomerates SM and Ayala — signals that ASEAN's positioning in the global technology governance conversation has moved beyond aspiration to active engagement. The presence of OpenAI's regional head alongside Google's is particularly notable: it suggests ASEAN is becoming a contested market for AI platform deployment, not merely a passive recipient of Silicon Valley products. The summit's timing, four days before the expected DEFA signing, is designed to build private-sector momentum behind the agreement's implementation. The challenge is translating summit declarations into on-the-ground impact for the 70 million MSMEs that account for over 90 per cent of ASEAN businesses — many of which still lack basic digital payment infrastructure, let alone AI capabilities. The Philippines' own MSME sector, while large, faces persistent barriers in access to finance and digital literacy that summit speeches alone cannot resolve.

Good evening. We turn now to Indonesia with this report. The Jakarta Composite Index closed at 5,115 on 11 November, down 10.70 points or 0.21 per cent, as investors adopted a cautious stance ahead of MSCI's semi-annual index rebalancing announcement scheduled for Thursday 12 November. The index opened higher at 5,139 and touched an intraday high of 5,145 before selling pressure pushed it to a low of 5,086. Total market capitalisation stood at 8,785 trillion rupiah. Of 945 stocks traded, 446 declined, 218 advanced, and 281 were unchanged. Basic materials led sectoral declines with a 1.86 per cent drop, followed by industrials at 0.93 per cent. Only transportation, energy and consumer staples closed in positive territory. The previous session had seen the IHSG rally 1.02 per cent to close at 5,126, with foreign investors recording net purchases of 172 billion rupiah. However, several major stocks continued to see foreign selling, led by Barito Pacific at 51 billion rupiah in net sales, Telkom Indonesia at 48 billion, and Barito Renewables Energy at 41 billion. Analysts noted that the MSCI rebalancing could result in additional Indonesian stocks being removed from the MSCI Emerging Markets Index, following the index provider's ongoing concerns about market transparency and governance.

Our analysis: The IHSG's inability to hold gains above 5,125 despite a broad rally the previous day underscores the deep uncertainty weighing on Indonesia's equity market ahead of the MSCI announcement. The rebalancing event scheduled for 12 November is distinct from the broader reclassification consultation — this is a routine semi-annual index review that will determine which Indonesian stocks remain in or are added to MSCI's investable indices. However, the two processes are intertwined: any further removals of Indonesian constituents would reinforce the narrative that Indonesia's capital market is shrinking within global indices rather than growing. The pattern of foreign selling in Barito-linked stocks and blue chips suggests institutional investors are pre-positioning for potential exclusions. With cumulative foreign net selling exceeding 98 trillion rupiah year-to-date and the IHSG down approximately 45 per cent from its January peak, Indonesia's market is operating under a level of sustained institutional pressure rarely seen in ASEAN emerging markets.

English presenter script

Good evening. We turn now to Malaysia with this report. The World Bank launched its October 2026 Malaysia Economic Monitor on 11 November, titled "From Quantity to Quality: Financing Malaysia's Productive SMEs," warning that the country must deepen structural reforms to sustain its trajectory toward high-income status. Zafer Mustafaoglu, the World Bank's division director for the Philippines, Malaysia and Brunei, said Malaysia's economy remained positive but faces significant external headwinds. He called on policymakers to rebuild fiscal space to strengthen resilience against future shocks and to keep monetary and macroprudential policy appropriately calibrated. The report's central finding is that Malaysia's SME sector must shift its financing from basic working capital toward higher-risk expansion capital — including funding for global market scaling, automation, digitalisation, AI adoption, and green transitions. Separately, HSBC has upgraded Malaysia's 2026 GDP growth forecast to 5.5 per cent, while the OECD's latest economic survey highlighted the need for continued fiscal consolidation alongside investment-friendly reform.

Our analysis: The World Bank's framing of Malaysia's reform imperative as "not optional" carries particular weight given the country's current macro-economic performance. Malaysia is delivering 5.3 per cent GDP growth with 1.8 per cent inflation — numbers that could easily breed complacency. The report's focus on SME financing quality rather than quantity is a subtle but important shift: Malaysia's SMEs can generally access credit, but the available financing is overwhelmingly short-term working capital rather than the patient, risk-tolerant capital needed for technology adoption and export scaling. This matters because Malaysia's stated goal of reaching high-income status by 2028 depends on productivity gains that only come from innovation and automation — areas where SMEs, which employ over 60 per cent of the workforce, are lagging. The timing also coincides with the ASEAN summit's DEFA discussions, which will impose new digital economy compliance requirements that Malaysian SMEs will need capital to meet. The Bank's call to rebuild fiscal space is a diplomatic way of saying the RON95 fuel subsidy rationalisation cannot be deferred indefinitely.

English presenter script

Good evening. We turn now to Vietnam with this report. Vietnam's economy grew an estimated 9.95 per cent year-on-year in the third quarter of 2026, accelerating from 8.81 per cent in Q2 and 8.15 per cent in Q1, according to the General Statistics Office. This marks the fastest quarterly growth rate since 2022 and brings the nine-month average to 9.01 per cent — placing Vietnam firmly on track as the fastest-growing major economy in ASEAN. Industry and construction led the expansion with 12.50 per cent growth, contributing 51.57 per cent of total gross value added. Services expanded 9.54 per cent while agriculture, forestry and fisheries grew 4.21 per cent. On the demand side, exports of goods and services surged 23.27 per cent, gross capital formation rose 21.39 per cent, and final consumption grew 8.96 per cent. GSO Director Nguyen Thi Huong credited strong government direction and said ministries and localities are working to achieve double-digit growth for the full year 2026. Twelve of thirty-four provinces and cities recorded growth of at least 10 per cent in the first nine months, led by Quang Ninh at 12.54 per cent, Ha Tinh at 12.36 per cent, and Hai Phong at 12.08 per cent. UOB has separately raised its full-year 2026 forecast for Vietnam to 8.5 per cent.

Our analysis: Vietnam's 9.95 per cent Q3 growth rate places it in rarefied territory — not just within ASEAN, where it is growing at roughly four times Thailand's rate, but globally, where only India among large economies is posting comparable expansion. The acceleration from 8.15 per cent in Q1 to 9.95 per cent in Q3 is driven by the manufacturing export cycle reaching full capacity: the 23.27 per cent surge in goods and services exports reflects factories that were ramping up component imports earlier in the year now shipping finished products. The geographic concentration of growth in northern industrial provinces — Quang Ninh, Hai Phong, Bac Ninh — maps directly onto the electronics and precision manufacturing corridor that has attracted Samsung, LG, and their supplier networks. The government's double-digit growth target for 2026 is ambitious but not unrealistic: achieving it would require Q4 growth above 12 per cent, which is possible if export momentum holds through the holiday ordering season. The risk is that the same concentration that produces spectacular headline growth also leaves southern and agricultural provinces behind — the gap between Quang Ninh at 12.54 per cent and Son La at 5.59 per cent illustrates a development divide that rapid national growth can mask but not resolve.

English presenter script

Good evening. We continue with Vietnam for a second report. Foreign direct investment inflows into Vietnam reached US$54.85 billion in the first ten months of 2026, up 74 per cent year-on-year — the highest level since 2009, according to official data. The total comprised US$30.73 billion in new project registrations, US$16.13 billion in additional capital for existing projects, and US$7.99 billion in capital contributions and share purchases. A total of 3,478 new projects were licensed, up 4.7 per cent in number, with registered capital more than doubling. Manufacturing and processing dominated new registrations at US$14.61 billion or 47.5 per cent, followed by transportation and warehousing at US$5.15 billion. By source country, Singapore led with US$9.52 billion or 31 per cent, followed by South Korea at US$5.81 billion, Luxembourg at US$4.99 billion, Hong Kong at US$3.57 billion, China at US$2.77 billion, and Japan at US$1.58 billion. Critically, disbursed FDI — the measure of actual capital deployment — reached US$24.08 billion, up 13.1 per cent year-on-year and the highest ten-month figure in five years. Manufacturing absorbed 82.9 per cent of disbursed capital at US$19.96 billion, confirming that foreign investors are actively building and operating factories rather than merely registering intentions.

Our analysis: The 74 per cent surge in registered FDI tells a compelling story about Vietnam's positioning in global supply chain reconfiguration. Singapore's dominance as the top source at 31 per cent reflects the city-state's role as a conduit for multinational investment — many of these flows represent third-country capital routed through Singaporean holding structures. South Korea's second-place position is directly linked to the Samsung-LG electronics manufacturing ecosystem that has made northern Vietnam a global production hub. The Luxembourg figure almost certainly represents European private equity and corporate restructuring flows rather than direct manufacturing investment. The most important figure, however, is the US$24.08 billion in disbursed FDI — up 13.1 per cent — because it measures what investors are actually spending on the ground. The gap between registered and disbursed FDI reflects the reality that many registered projects take years to deploy capital fully, and some never do. Vietnam's challenge is converting the historically high registration pipeline into actual factories, jobs, and technology transfer — and doing so in higher-value segments rather than remaining an assembly platform.

English presenter script

Good evening. We turn now to the ASEAN region with this report. The ten ASEAN member states are expected to sign the Digital Economy Framework Agreement during the 49th ASEAN Summit in Manila on 15 to 17 November, marking the conclusion of negotiations that were formally completed in October 2026. DEFA is described as the world's first binding regional digital economy agreement, covering cross-border data flows, digital payments, e-commerce facilitation, cybersecurity cooperation, and AI governance for a market of approximately 670 million people. The agreement was a signature priority of the Philippines' 2026 ASEAN chairship. Rajah and Tann Asia noted that negotiations were concluded ahead of the November target, while ANTARA News Agency reported that ASEAN economic ministers had targeted the signing for the November summit. DEFA's scope extends beyond traditional trade agreements to include provisions on digital identity, consumer protection in e-commerce, competition policy for digital platforms, and a framework for emerging technologies including artificial intelligence. The agreement will require ratification by individual member states before its provisions take full legal effect, a process that typically takes twelve to twenty-four months across ASEAN.

Our analysis: DEFA's expected signing would be a genuine milestone for ASEAN — no other regional bloc has achieved a binding digital economy framework of this scope. The European Union's Digital Markets Act and Digital Services Act regulate digital platforms but are single-jurisdiction instruments; DEFA attempts to harmonise rules across ten sovereign nations with vastly different levels of digital development. This ambition is also its greatest challenge: Singapore's digital economy operates at a fundamentally different level from Myanmar's or Cambodia's. The agreement's provisions on cross-border data flows will be particularly contentious in implementation, as several ASEAN members — notably Vietnam and Indonesia — maintain strict data localisation requirements that conflict with the free-flow principles DEFA espouses. The ratification timeline of twelve to twenty-four months means the earliest substantive impact would be felt in 2028, by which time the AI governance provisions may already require updating given the pace of technology change. The real test will be whether DEFA creates a genuinely integrated digital single market or becomes another aspirational framework that sets targets without mechanisms to enforce them.

Good evening. We close tonight's bulletin with a second report from Indonesia. The Indonesia Stock Exchange removed 82 stocks classified as having high shareholding concentration from the Jakarta Composite Index effective 2 November, reducing the index's constituents from 963 to 881. The reform directly addresses concerns raised by MSCI about ownership transparency and concentrated shareholding patterns. The excluded stocks had collectively accounted for approximately 18 per cent of the IHSG's weighting and roughly 30 per cent of the exchange's total market capitalisation. Notable removals include DCI Indonesia, controlled by Otto Toto Sugiri, with a market capitalisation of approximately 417 trillion rupiah; Bayan Resources, controlled by Low Tuck Kwong, at approximately 305 trillion; Barito Renewables Energy, affiliated with Prajogo Pangestu, at approximately 240 trillion; as well as several Sinar Mas-linked issuers. IDX President Director Jeffrey Hendrik said the weightings of removed stocks would be redistributed among remaining constituents. The HSC classification was determined as of 22 October, and the IHSG's starting level was unchanged at the prior close of 5,057, as the removal was handled through a reweighting rather than a level reset.

Our analysis: The removal of 82 HSC stocks is the most aggressive single-day compositional reform in the IHSG's history and represents Indonesia's most tangible response to MSCI's governance concerns. By excising stocks where a small number of shareholders control the overwhelming majority of shares — leaving insufficient free float for genuine price discovery — the exchange is attempting to demonstrate that it takes market integrity seriously. The inclusion of marquee names like DCI Indonesia, Bayan Resources and Barito Renewables signals that no issuer is exempt from the new transparency regime, even those with market capitalisations exceeding 200 trillion rupiah. However, the reform has a painful side effect: the excluded stocks accounted for 30 per cent of total market capitalisation, meaning the IHSG now represents a substantially smaller slice of Indonesia's listed equity universe. This raises questions about whether institutional investors will treat the reformed index as a more investable benchmark or a diminished one. The timing — just ten days before the MSCI November rebalancing announcement — suggests IDX is making a last-minute case that Indonesia's market reform commitment is credible.
