Good evening. We open tonight with the 49th ASEAN Summit, which formally commenced in Manila today with all ten ASEAN leaders in attendance alongside a roster of global heavyweights including US President Donald Trump, Chinese Premier Li Qiang, and Japanese Prime Minister Shigeru Ishiba. Philippine President Ferdinand Marcos Jr., in his capacity as ASEAN chair, declared that the bloc has become "a strong regional bloc that can weather global headwinds" — a statement aimed at projecting confidence amid escalating trade tensions and geopolitical rivalries. Day one of the summit addressed three critical agenda items. First, leaders agreed on a conditional pathway for Myanmar's re-engagement with the bloc — the most assertive posture ASEAN has adopted on the Myanmar crisis since the 2021 coup. Second, leaders acknowledged that the self-imposed 2026 deadline for completing the Code of Conduct in the South China Sea has been missed, with negotiations to continue into 2027 under Singapore's incoming chairship. Third, leaders celebrated the signing of the Digital Economy Framework Agreement the day before, hailing it as proof that ASEAN can deliver binding economic agreements. Separately, the Philippines pitched 1.5 billion US dollars in investment-ready projects at the ASEAN Investment Forum, positioning the country as an emerging hub for regional capital flows.

Our analysis: The Manila summit underscores why ASEAN, with its combined GDP approaching four trillion dollars and a population of 670 million, has become impossible for the world's major powers to ignore. The presence of Trump, Li Qiang, and Ishiba at the same table reflects the intensifying strategic competition for influence in Southeast Asia — a dynamic that gives ASEAN leverage but also exposes it to being pulled in competing directions. The conditional pathway for Myanmar represents ASEAN's strongest collective stance on the crisis to date, moving beyond the ineffective five-point consensus toward a framework that ties Myanmar's return to verifiable progress on cessation of hostilities. Whether this proves to be a genuine turning point or another aspirational statement depends entirely on the enforcement mechanisms, which remain undefined. The missed South China Sea Code of Conduct deadline, meanwhile, is a sobering reminder that after more than twenty years of negotiations, fundamental disagreements between China and the claimant states on legal force, geographic scope, and enforcement remain unresolved. The summit's economic deliverables — DEFA, the Canada trade agreements, the investment forum — are the clearest proof that ASEAN's value extends well beyond its role as a security dialogue platform, providing tangible commercial incentives that keep even reluctant partners engaged.

Good evening. We turn now to Malaysia with this report. Malaysia is on track to record GDP growth exceeding five per cent in 2026, which would make it three consecutive years of above-five-per-cent expansion — the country's most sustained period of strong growth since before the 2008 global financial crisis. Finance Minister Amir Hamzah expressed confidence in the outlook, citing quarterly results that have consistently beaten expectations: 5.4 per cent growth in the first quarter and 5.3 per cent in the third quarter. This builds on 5.2 per cent growth recorded in 2025 and 5.1 per cent in 2024, marking a progressive strengthening under Prime Minister Anwar Ibrahim's administration. The growth has been powered by a wave of technology investment commitments, with Microsoft, Google, and Amazon Web Services all announcing major data centre and cloud infrastructure investments in Malaysia over the past two years. Meanwhile, Malaysia's headline inflation averaged just 1.8 per cent — the lowest in ASEAN — creating a benign environment for both consumers and businesses. The semiconductor sector, in which Malaysia holds a significant share of global outsourced assembly and test capacity, has been a particular beneficiary of the global AI hardware buildout.

Our analysis: Three consecutive years of above-five-per-cent growth represents a genuine economic achievement for Malaysia and a vindication of Prime Minister Anwar Ibrahim's economic management narrative. The consistency is notable — this is not a single-quarter spike driven by a base effect or one-off factor, but a sustained multi-year trajectory supported by diversified drivers including domestic consumption, public investment, and technology-sector capital inflows. However, there are concentration risks that merit attention. The data centre and semiconductor investment wave, while transformative, is heavily correlated with the global AI investment cycle. If AI infrastructure spending were to slow — whether due to corporate capital discipline, technological plateaus, or macroeconomic headwinds — Malaysia's pipeline of committed but not yet operational projects could thin rapidly. The political dimension is also significant: PM Anwar can credibly point to this growth record as evidence that his Madani Economy framework is delivering results, strengthening his domestic political position ahead of future elections. Yet the World Bank's recent call for 'decisive reforms' — in areas including fuel subsidies, labour markets, and education — serves as a reminder that sustaining this performance beyond the current favourable cycle will require structural adjustments that carry political costs. The question for Malaysia is whether it uses this period of strength to make those difficult reforms, or whether the very success of the current model reduces the incentive to change.

Good evening. We turn now to Singapore with this report. Singapore's economy expanded 5.4 per cent year on year in the third quarter of 2026, according to advance estimates from the Department of Statistics — a robust figure, though representing a moderation from the 6.2 per cent growth recorded in the second quarter. On a quarter-on-quarter seasonally adjusted basis, the economy grew 1.1 per cent. The primary engine of growth continues to be the artificial intelligence infrastructure boom. Our analysis: Singapore's 5.4 per cent third-quarter growth, while a step down from Q2, remains well above the city-state's long-run potential of roughly two to three per cent, confirming that the AI-driven structural shift is delivering a meaningful growth premium. The critical insight is that this is not a broad-based boom — it is a concentrated acceleration in technology-adjacent sectors that is creating what analysts describe as a two-speed economy. Data centres, cloud services, semiconductor design, and AI-related financial advisory are surging, while more traditional sectors such as retail, food services, and non-technology manufacturing are growing at more modest rates. This bifurcation creates both opportunity and risk. The opportunity is that Singapore is cementing its position as Southeast Asia's premier AI infrastructure hub — a status that will attract talent, capital, and corporate headquarters for years to come. The risk is cyclical correlation: Singapore's growth premium is now heavily tied to global AI investment spending, which itself depends on corporate capital expenditure decisions at a handful of major technology companies. Any significant pullback in AI infrastructure spending — whether from demand saturation, regulatory intervention, or a shift in investor sentiment — would disproportionately affect Singapore relative to ASEAN peers with more diversified growth profiles. The Monetary Authority's cautious language about moderation in 2026 suggests policymakers are aware of this concentration risk even as they benefit from it.

English presenter script

Good evening. We turn now to Indonesia with this report. Indonesia has attracted 13.23 billion US dollars in foreign portfolio inflows during the first ten months of 2026, according to data compiled from Bank Indonesia and market sources, marking one of the strongest periods of capital attraction in the country's recent history. The inflows have been driven primarily by Bank Indonesia's Sekuritas Rupiah — known as SRBI — high-yield instruments, which offer attractive real returns to global investors, as well as renewed demand for Indonesian sovereign bonds. Notably, foreign investors returned to Indonesian government bonds for the first time in more than seven years, a signal of significantly improved confidence in the country's fiscal and monetary management. Bank Indonesia's series of rate hikes earlier in the year helped establish a yield premium that compensated international investors for currency risk on the rupiah. Indonesia's GDP growth also picked up pace in the third quarter, and the country's official reserve assets remained solid through October, providing additional reassurance to portfolio managers evaluating the country's ability to service external obligations and manage balance-of-payments dynamics.

Our analysis: The 13.23-billion-dollar portfolio inflow figure represents a remarkable turnaround from the capital outflow pressures Indonesia experienced during 2023 and the early months of 2024, when a strong US dollar and elevated American interest rates drew capital away from emerging markets globally. The SRBI instrument has proven to be a particularly effective innovation — by offering high-yield returns denominated in rupiah with relatively short maturities, Bank Indonesia created a product specifically calibrated to attract the kind of global yield-seeking capital that had bypassed Indonesia for several years. The return of foreign investors to sovereign bonds after a seven-year absence is perhaps the most symbolically significant element, as government bond markets are typically the benchmark for foreign confidence in a country's economic management. However, portfolio capital is inherently more volatile than foreign direct investment — it can exit as quickly as it entered, particularly during global risk-off episodes or if the yield differential narrows. Indonesia's challenge is to leverage this period of strong portfolio interest to attract more permanent forms of foreign investment — greenfield manufacturing, infrastructure, and technology projects — that create jobs and build productive capacity rather than simply flowing through financial instruments. Bank Indonesia's balancing act between maintaining attractive yields to retain portfolio capital and eventually easing monetary policy to support domestic economic growth will be one of the key policy tensions to watch across ASEAN in 2027.

Good evening. We turn now to the Philippines with this report. The Philippines is awaiting a formal response from Washington on the Pax Silica framework — an ambitious bilateral agreement that would establish a major semiconductor and artificial intelligence manufacturing hub at New Clark City in Tarlac, approximately 100 kilometres north of Manila. The proposed economic security zone spans 4,000 acres and is designed to anchor a new generation of chip packaging, AI hardware assembly, and advanced electronics manufacturing in the Philippines. The draft framework has been finalised by both sides, with Philippine officials indicating that only the US response remains outstanding. President Trump's visit to Manila for the ASEAN Summit creates a natural diplomatic window for advancing the agreement, although Philippine officials have stated that the summit itself is not the intended venue for a formal signing. Foxconn, the Taiwanese electronics manufacturing giant, has been identified as an anchor investor for the zone. Philippine electronics exports could reach a record 54 billion US dollars this year, and the Pax Silica initiative sits within the broader Luzon Economic Corridor — a trilateral US-Philippines-Japan strategic framework designed to strengthen supply chain resilience across the Indo-Pacific.

Our analysis: Pax Silica represents the most ambitious technology and industrial policy initiative the Philippines has undertaken in decades — a bet that the country can position itself as a critical node in the global semiconductor supply chain at precisely the moment when the United States, Japan, and their allies are seeking to reduce dependence on production concentrated in Taiwan and mainland China. The Philippines brings genuine advantages to this proposition: competitive labour costs, a large English-speaking workforce, established outsourced semiconductor assembly and test capabilities — known as OSAT — and a strategic location within the first island chain. However, the challenges are equally formidable. Power generation remains a binding constraint: the Philippines has some of the highest electricity costs in Asia, and semiconductor fabrication and data centres are extraordinarily energy-intensive. The skills pipeline requires massive expansion — moving from assembly to advanced packaging and eventually to fabrication demands a workforce trained in materials science, precision engineering, and cleanroom operations that the current education system does not produce at scale. There is also a historical pattern to reckon with: the Philippines has launched numerous special economic zones over the decades, and while some — particularly in electronics assembly — have succeeded, many others have struggled to translate policy ambitions into operational industrial clusters. The Bank of Thailand has raised its GDP growth forecast for 2026 to 2.4 per cent, while holding the policy interest rate steady at one per cent for the fourth consecutive meeting. The Joint Standing Committee on Commerce, Industry and Banking — known as JSCCIB — Thailand's principal business federation, separately raised its own growth forecast to a range of 2.4 to 2.6 per cent, reflecting improved confidence in the export sector. Our analysis: Thailand's 2.4 per cent growth forecast places it significantly behind its ASEAN peers — Vietnam at 8.2 per cent, Malaysia at above 5 per cent, Singapore at 5.4 per cent, and even Indonesia at approximately 5 per cent — raising uncomfortable questions about whether Thailand's economic model is structurally underperforming. The Bank of Thailand's decision to hold rates at one per cent for the fourth consecutive meeting reflects a genuine policy dilemma. Cutting rates would provide relief to domestic consumers and SMEs burdened by near-record household debt, but it would widen the interest rate differential with the United States, potentially accelerating capital outflows and putting pressure on the baht. Holding rates preserves financial stability but does little to address the domestic weakness that the central bank itself identifies as the economy's primary vulnerability. The ASEAN-focussed Macroeconomic Research Office — AMRO — has flagged Thailand's concentrated recovery as a risk, validating the Bank of Thailand's own assessment that the headline improvement masks a deeply uneven distribution of economic gains. For Prime Minister Anutin, the situation is politically sensitive: his coalition government came to power partly on promises of economic revitalisation, and a growth rate that trails every significant ASEAN neighbour makes it difficult to claim that promise is being fulfilled. The export boom, driven largely by the global electronics and automotive cycle, is welcome but insufficient to address the structural challenges — including household debt, an aging population, and insufficient domestic demand — that have made Thailand ASEAN's slowest-growing major economy for the better part of a decade.
