MSCI Launches Formal Consultation to Reclassify Indonesia from Emerging to Standalone Market

MSCI正式启动磋商，拟将印尼从新兴市场降级为独立市场

English presenter script

Good evening. We turn now to Indonesia with this report. On 15 October 2026, MSCI launched a formal public consultation on a proposal to reclassify Indonesia from Emerging Markets to Standalone Market status — a dramatic escalation from the index provider's June decision, which merely extended its review. The consultation document cited six areas of concern: opaque beneficial ownership structures, suspected coordinated trading patterns, inadequate regulatory enforcement, a lack of independent board oversight at listed companies, restrictions on short selling and securities lending, and a continued rebalancing freeze that has been in effect since March 2026. MSCI has proposed a two-step reclassification process: Indonesia would first move to Frontier Market status by November 2027, and potentially to Standalone Market status by May 2028. The consultation period runs until 15 January 2027, with a decision expected in February. The Jakarta Composite Index plunged approximately 11 per cent on 16 October in response, triggering a trading halt — the steepest single-day decline since the 2008 global financial crisis. As of 7 November, the IHSG stood at approximately 5,050, with foreign investors recording net selling of roughly 98 trillion rupiah year to date. The Financial Services Authority (OJK) and the Indonesia Stock Exchange (BEI) have announced they will hold direct discussions with MSCI to address the concerns raised.

Our analysis: The shift from a review extension in June to a formal reclassification consultation represents a qualitative escalation that markets have clearly registered. Standalone status would remove Indonesia from all major MSCI indices, potentially triggering between US$8 billion and US$12 billion in passive fund outflows as index-tracking funds are forced to sell. The proposed two-step timeline gives Indonesia approximately twelve months to demonstrate meaningful reform before the first downgrade takes effect. The IHSG's 45 per cent decline from its January peak suggests investors are already pricing in significant downgrade risk. There is a painful paradox at work: the market selloff itself complicates the reform effort by reducing liquidity, discouraging new listings, and undermining the financial viability of the very market infrastructure improvements that MSCI is demanding. Indonesia's real economy remains sound — GDP growth above 5 per cent — making this one of the starkest divergences between market performance and economic fundamentals in recent emerging-market history.

Good evening. We turn now to the Philippines with this report. The Philippines is in the final stages of preparation for the 49th ASEAN Summit, scheduled for 15 to 17 November in Manila. More than 300 domestic and international flights have been cancelled or rescheduled to accommodate the event, while the Armed Forces of the Philippines has deployed a full security operation across the capital. The summit is shaping up to be one of the largest diplomatic gatherings in Philippine history: confirmed attendees include leaders of all ASEAN member states — with Myanmar represented by a career diplomat — alongside U.S. President Donald Trump, Canadian Prime Minister Mark Carney, Brazilian President Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva, and at least fourteen other world leaders. Our analysis: The scale of Manila's mobilisation reflects both the summit's diplomatic significance and the Philippines' determination to use its 2026 ASEAN chairship as a platform for economic re-engagement. The cancellation of over 300 flights and the deployment of military security are necessary for an event of this magnitude, but they impose real costs on Manila's economy and its residents. The ASEAN Inclusive Growth Summit's corporate lineup — spanning fintech, conglomerates, and artificial intelligence — signals that ASEAN is actively positioning itself in global technology governance conversations. If DEFA is signed as expected, it would represent a genuine milestone: a binding digital economy framework that no other regional bloc has achieved. The challenge for the Philippines specifically is translating this diplomatic success into tangible economic benefits, given four consecutive quarters of slowing growth, falling foreign direct investment, and an unresolved energy crisis that continues to weigh on business sentiment.

Good evening. We turn now to Malaysia with this report. Bank Negara Malaysia held its overnight policy rate at 2.75 per cent on 5 November, the final Monetary Policy Committee meeting of 2026, in line with market expectations. Governor Abdul Rasheed Ghaffour stated explicitly that there is no need to tighten monetary policy, given the economy's firm footing. The rate has been unchanged since September 2025, marking more than a year of stability. Malaysia's economy delivered GDP growth of 5.3 per cent in the third quarter, beating market expectations and confirming broad-based momentum. Headline inflation averaged just 1.8 per cent year on year — the lowest in ASEAN — with core inflation running at approximately 2 per cent. The central bank acknowledged external risks, including trade policy uncertainty and geopolitical tensions, but said the balance of risks to growth is tilted to the upside. Our analysis: The decision confirms Malaysia's position as the most macro-stable economy in ASEAN. The governor's explicit statement that there is no need to tighten pushes back against HSBC's earlier call for a 25 basis point hike in the first quarter of 2027. However, the longer Malaysia sustains above-trend growth without policy adjustment, the greater the risk of inflation building — particularly if the ringgit weakens or global energy prices rise. The real test of this equilibrium will come when the government addresses the RON95 fuel subsidy, which costs billions of ringgit annually. Any phase-out or restructuring of the subsidy could introduce a one-off inflation shock that would force Bank Negara's hand on rates.

English presenter script

Good evening. We turn now to Singapore with this report. The Monetary Authority of Singapore kept its exchange rate-based policy band unchanged on 14 October, maintaining the prevailing rate of appreciation of the Singapore dollar nominal effective exchange rate with no change to the band's width or centre. The decision surprised all ten economists surveyed, each of whom had expected a tightening move. It came alongside third-quarter GDP growth of 5.4 per cent year on year — down from 6.2 per cent in the second quarter but comfortably beating the consensus forecast of approximately 4.7 per cent. On a seasonally adjusted quarter-on-quarter basis, the economy expanded 3.2 per cent, accelerating sharply from just 0.4 per cent in the previous quarter. The MAS narrowed its core inflation forecast for 2026 to 1.5 to 2.0 per cent, from a previous range of 1.5 to 2.5 per cent. MUFG said the hold signals an 'extended pause,' while DBS noted that the authority appears comfortable that inflation is on a sustainable downward trajectory. By sector, manufacturing grew 7.5 per cent year on year, driven by electronics and precision engineering, while services expanded 5.0 per cent.

Our analysis: This may mark an inflection point in Singapore's monetary policy cycle. After tightening five times since October 2021, the MAS appears to be signalling that its inflation fight is effectively over. The narrowed core inflation forecast — cutting the upper bound by half a percentage point — amounts to a declaration of victory on price stability. The decision not to tighten despite 5.4 per cent growth suggests the authority is now prioritising export competitiveness over further inflation reduction. This has regional spillover implications: a less-appreciated Singapore dollar reduces competitive pressure on neighbouring currencies, giving other ASEAN central banks — particularly Bank Negara Malaysia and Bank of Thailand — additional room to maintain their own accommodative stances. The principal risk is that AI-driven investment demand, which has been a major growth engine, could reignite inflation through commercial real estate demand for data centres without generating corresponding improvements in broader economic welfare.

English presenter script

Good evening. We turn now to Thailand with this report. The Bank of Thailand raised its 2026 GDP growth forecast to 2.4 per cent from 2.3 per cent at its October Monetary Policy Committee meeting, while also lifting its projection for 2027. The upgrade is driven primarily by stronger-than-expected export performance and a continued recovery in tourism arrivals. However, Governor Sethaput Suthiwartnarueput offered a notably candid assessment, warning that the recovery 'remains concentrated' in external-facing sectors and has not translated into broad-based domestic demand. He described the overall growth picture as 'not good' but resilient to external shocks. The policy rate was held at 1.00 per cent for a fourth consecutive meeting — making Thailand the most accommodative major economy in ASEAN. The Thai Chamber of Commerce has also raised its growth forecast, citing similar export-driven momentum. The SET Index has been trading in a range of approximately 1,460 to 1,500 in early November, unable to sustain rallies above technical resistance levels.

Our analysis: At 2.4 per cent, Thailand's growth is less than half that of Malaysia at 5.5 per cent or Vietnam at 9 per cent — and Governor Sethaput's 'not good' characterisation is remarkably candid for a central bank chief. The concentration of recovery in exports and tourism, rather than domestic consumption, suggests that the 1 per cent policy rate has reached the limits of what monetary easing alone can achieve. Thailand's household debt, which exceeds 90 per cent of GDP, constrains consumer spending even at very low interest rates — households are servicing existing debt rather than taking on new borrowing to spend. The SET Index's inability to sustain rallies above the 1,500 level suggests that equity investors see the domestic economy as structurally stalled. Without meaningful reforms to address household debt burdens, energy costs, and bureaucratic inefficiency, Thailand risks settling into a prolonged low-growth trap that monetary policy cannot resolve.

English presenter script

Good evening. We turn now to Vietnam with this report. Vietnam posted a second consecutive monthly trade surplus in October, ending a run of nine deficit months that had raised concerns about the sustainability of the country's export-led growth model. Total merchandise trade for the first ten months of 2026 reached US$1,010.55 billion, up 32.3 per cent year on year, confirming the historic breach of the trillion-dollar threshold. Exports totalled US$499.24 billion while imports stood at US$511.31 billion. The Ministry of Industry and Trade has called for greater market diversification, noting that Vietnam's heavy reliance on the United States — with which it runs a US$140 billion surplus — creates significant vulnerability to trade policy shifts. Japanese investors are signalling renewed interest in Vietnam across manufacturing, technology, and green energy sectors, providing a potential counterweight to the concentration risk. Meanwhile, Vietnam's utilisation rate for the Comprehensive and Progressive Agreement for Trans-Pacific Partnership remains below 10 per cent, though officials say it is improving as businesses become more familiar with the agreement's rules of origin requirements.

Our analysis: The return to monthly surpluses signals that the mid-2026 deficit scare was cyclical rather than structural. Vietnam's trade pattern follows a predictable sequence: imports of components and raw materials surge first as factories ramp up production, and finished goods exports then produce surpluses with a lag. The US$140 billion surplus with the United States, however, makes Vietnam a conspicuous target for trade rebalancing pressure. The Section 301 duties imposed in July 2026 — adding 12.5 per cent on Vietnamese-origin products — are a tangible warning.
